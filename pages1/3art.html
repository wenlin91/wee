How can we make sure AI-written blogs are any good?

👤 Author: Alini📅 Date: 18/08/2026⏱ Reading time: 10-20 mins

1. Clear prompts, steering it from the start

When blogging with AI, the main worry is thinking it’s a magic machine. Just typing something like "Write a good article about reading," and expecting a treat. But what you get is like a cup of warm water—you can drink it whenever, but it’s completely tasteless. Where's the problem? It's because the words you used are too vague. No matter how clever AI is, it still can't picture what that vague word for "good" in your mind actually looks like. If you ask it to guess, it can only give an average result—not wrong, but not impressive.

So, controlling the output depends entirely on how well you craft your prompts. I've now got into a habit: talking to AI is like giving tasks to a new intern. I wouldn't just say "write a running blog," but rather "write a running experience for a 35-year-old office worker, focusing on how to ease back and waist pain during morning runs, casual tone like chatting with a friend, not too sentimental, not overly professional, and finish with a real-life feeling." Look, this passage contains who is watching, why they're watching, what tone, what structure, and where the focus lies. When AI gets this, at least what comes out is heading in the right direction. It's no longer casting a net aimlessly, but going with the flow of the river you draw.

The more precise the prompts, the less you need to tweak later. This isn't about restricting AI, but about saving it effort and saving you time. When the source is tightened, the water won't run wild.

2. Facts must be thorough, keeping the focus on quality.

AI has a quirk—it’s really good at talking rubbish seriously. Writing a scene description is fine, but once it comes to specific numbers, names, times, places, or historical events, it starts 'making things up.' Sometimes it mixes two unrelated things together, and sometimes it just invents a scenario that seems quite similar. Since then, I've set a strict rule: for all factual information given by AI, if it involves time, place, data, personal names, or institution names, it must be manually checked. It's easy to verify—just a two-minute search. But don't try to cut those two minutes short. If you slack off, you could end up spending a couple of days explaining it to readers. Sticking to the core facts is the basic way of respecting your readers. You can write casually, but you can't make mistakes. This is the lifeblood of blogging—no one can trust you otherwise.

3. Add your own content to avoid it feeling too AI-generated

You start to notice a pattern the more AI stuff you read—it just feels too 'perfect.' Sentences flow so smoothly it doesn’t sound like natural human speech, and the start and end of each piece are so neatly aligned it’s like they’ve been moulded. Paragraphs are all about the same length and every sentence is spot on. This kind of ‘perfection’ actually makes people suspicious, more like a manual than someone actually chatting with you.

How do you get rid of this AI vibe? There's only one way: mix in your own interests. Every time I get the AI's first draft, I don't just change words or sentences directly. Instead, I read through it once, find the 'too smooth' parts, and then insert my own ideas. For example, if AI writes 'Consistently running can make people feel better,' I would change it to 'Consistently running can improve my mood. At least by the third day, I stopped honking wildly in traffic.' Add 'me,' add 'third day,' add 'honk'—it's the details, details are originality, originality is human touch.

There's another trick: deliberately leave a bit of imperfection. For example, sentences of varying lengths, occasionally adding a colloquial sentiment, or even inserting parentheses to add a heartfelt message. These 'rough edges' are exactly what AI dares not write, because it aims for high accuracy, whereas humans write with emotion. Only blogs with emotion make people want to keep reading.

4. Human-machine collaboration to set up a final review system.

No matter how powerful AI is, it's just a tool. If you ask it to write the first draft, it does it quickly and well; if you polish the paragraphs, it can also revise smoothly. But there's one thing it can never do—make decisions for you.

What does 'final decision' mean? It means when you read through the entire text one last time, you can bang the table and say, 'That's it, I accept this.' This word 'recognise' includes your assurance of the authenticity of the content, your control over tone, and your commitment to the core viewpoints. All of these things are yours; AI can't replace a single bit of them.

So my final review process is simple: every article that AI has contributed to must be read aloud by me from start to finish before publishing. The advantage of reading aloud is that those AI-written "standard sentence patterns" sound smooth but lack emotion, so I just cut them out and rewrite them; for the details I added myself, if there are stumbles, pauses or breathing sounds, I keep them.

Sometimes, as you read, you realise a certain passage's logic doesn't hold up—I didn't notice it when the AI wrote it, nor did I notice it when I read it myself, but as soon as I read it out loud, it was exposed. This 'read aloud' action is the final security checkpoint.

There's also a rule for the final review: if you're still not happy after three revisions, just put it aside and read it tomorrow. Time is the best way to calm things down. When your mind is clear and you go back to read, the layer of 'grudge' from AI will show up on its own, then just cut it away cleanly.

The essence of human-machine collaboration has never been about one replacing the other, but about each doing what they do best. AI takes care of paving roads and bridges, while you're in charge of overseeing and building monuments. No matter how smooth the road is, you have to carve the words on the stele yourself; if it's crooked, it's on you. This might sound like a burden, but it's actually a confidence booster—because you take responsibility for every word you write, readers will want to keep reading.
